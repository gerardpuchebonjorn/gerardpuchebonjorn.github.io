Basic fundamentals for mechanistic interpretability

In recent years deep learning has become exceedingly good at predicting several types of data from training with huge amounts of data and clever post-training. The best example of it is of course text, with LLMs, but there are others, such as convolutional neural networks for computer vision or the transformer architecture for protein structure prediction.

One of the main reasons these models have become so good is scale: we enable them to have billions or even trillions of parameters, so that there is space to learn as many patterns as possible. There’s a trade-off though, we stop knowing how exactly the model is producing its outcome. I mean, we know about every single matrix multiplication and activation function, but we don’t understand the decision-making process. Trying to do so is what mechanistic interpretability attempts.

fundamentals

First let’s see an example where we can clearly see the decision-making process in a machine learning model. The best example I can think of is decision trees. Consider this old typical model to classify potential breast-cancer tumors as benign or malignant. We get the following dataset:

Then, we can train a decision tree classifier. The main idea is: look at every feature and every possible threshold, and pick the single yes/no question (“is worst radius ≤ 16.80?”) that best separates the malignant tumors from the benign ones. “Best” just means that after the split, each side is as close as possible to being all malignant or all benign. We split the data by that question, and then do the same thing again inside each half, until the groups are pure or we hit a maximum depth. Here I kept 426 tumors for training and 143 for testing, with a maximum depth of 2.

So we can clearly see how the model is deciding to classify the tumor as benign or malignant, you can literally see the chain of decisions for any input. If the worst radius is ≤ 16.80 and the worst concave points are ≤ 0.14, it is benign. If the worst radius is ≤ 16.80 but the worst concave points are above 0.14, it is malignant. If the worst radius is above 16.80, it is malignant, unless the texture error is ≤ 0.47, in which case it is benign.

Now let us look at another model, an LLM, we can use gemma-2-2b as an example. What happens when the model receives the text below and has to predict the next word (token)?

The actual truthfulness of the justice system in the US is _

Its whole job is to guess what comes in place of the _.

In order to understand this we shall go through a bit of a breakdown of the architecture.

from words to tokens

The first thing is to understand that our model sees tokens, not words. Tokens are simply the text units of our model: a token can be a whole word, but also only part of one, punctuation, a blank space, etc. Each of these tokens has an ID, and this ID expands into a vector, in our case with 2,304 entries. (See a breakdown of LLMs if you want to go a bit further on tokenization.)

This vector serves as a way to embed the words: we train our model so that these vectors live in a mathematical space that makes some semantic sense, for instance the vector (king − queen) ≈ (man − woman). Again, this only happens after training.

This token embedding will be our input (a matrix made of our token vectors), so now we can start looking at the model. The general picture is that we pass the embedding through 26 layers (repetitions, more or less) of an attention block and an MLP (two big mathematical functions). After that some normalization is done and we get probabilities for the next word (recall we are predicting the next word of: “The actual truthfulness of the justice system in the US is _”).

tokens talk to each other: attention

Take the vector for “is”. On its own it knows nothing about the rest of the sentence. Attention updates it by adding a weighted mix of the vectors of the words before it. Some words get much more weight than others: here “justice”, “system” and “US” matter a lot, “the” and “of” barely at all. Which words get more weight is what the model learns in training.

thinking on its own: the MLP

Then each token goes through the MLP on its own, without looking at the others. This is where the model recalls what it learned in training, a bit like facts about a word: when “US” goes in, a few of its 9,216 neurons light up and add what it knows about the US to its vector.

Each block adds its result to the token’s vector. Attention, then MLP, is one layer; Gemma repeats it 26 times.

picking the next word

After the last layer, the vector of “is” is used to give a score to each of the 256,000 tokens in the vocabulary. Those scores are turned into probabilities that add up to 100%.

For our prompt, the model’s favourite next word is “a” (13%), then “being” and “not”. It picks one, usually at random following these chances, adds it to the text, and runs again for the next word.

So everything the model works out about our sentence lives in those vectors, layer after layer. This means that, for example, we can stop at any layer and look at the vectors there.
