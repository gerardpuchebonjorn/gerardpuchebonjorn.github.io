Creativity in diffusion models

Diffusion models have always struck me as something quite curious. The main idea is:

Take an image and add a little bit of random noise to it, then a bit more, and keep going for many steps until nothing is left but noise. Then train a neural network to do the opposite: given a noisy image, remove a little of the noise. Once it has learned that, we can start from pure noise and apply it over and over, and step by step an image appears.

The first thing that came to mind is that if you do this, then why do new images arise? We should simply go back to the training ones. It is true that we can start from "new noise" and see what the neural net comes up with, but still the outcome of this felt a bit ambiguous.

A recent paper from Google, “On the Interpolation Effect of Score Smoothing in Diffusion Models” by Zhengdao Chen (ICLR 2026, see also the blog post), went into creativity in diffusion models and I thought it was a good exercise to break it down.

what the network actually learns

When the network looks at a noisy image, what it really learns is an arrow: from where the noisy image is, which direction should it move to look a bit more like real data. This arrow is called the score. If we think of every image as a point in a huge space (one coordinate per pixel), the score is a field of arrows over that whole space, and it changes with the noise level: at high noise the arrows are rough and point to the general area where the data lives, at low noise they point very precisely.

In the paper’s notation, with noise level t, the score at a point x is

score(x) = (x̂(x) − x) / t

where x̂(x) is the network’s best guess of the clean image behind x. So the arrow simply points from x towards that guess, and the smaller the noise, the stronger the pull. Generating an image means starting from noise and following the arrows while the noise level goes down to zero.

the problem: the perfect score memorizes

Training minimizes a loss whose best possible answer is known exactly. We only have a finite training set, so the best guess x̂(x) is a weighted average of the training images, weighted by how likely each one is to have produced x after adding noise. The score built from this is called the empirical score function, and it can be written down in closed form, no network needed.

The catch is what happens as the noise goes to zero. The weights become all-or-nothing: the closest training image gets almost all of the weight, so every arrow points straight at one specific training image. Following these arrows exactly undoes the noise we added to the training set, so we end up with the training set again:

So a model that learned the score perfectly would just be a complicated way of retrieving its training data. This is not only theory: memorization does show up in practice when models are very large compared to their training set. Since real diffusion models do produce new images, the network must be learning the score imperfectly, and the question becomes what kind of imperfection gives creativity instead of noise.
