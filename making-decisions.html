on making decisions

in a bit of an ironic way, I think the best advice on decisions I have heard is from Hinton:

“I don’t know if I’m in a good position to give advice, but the piece of advice I normally give is, if you have an idea and it seems right to you and it’s different from what everybody else believes, don’t give up on it until you’ve figured out why it’s wrong. Most ideas you have like that, you are wrong. There’s something you haven’t thought of or there’s something you didn’t understand. Just very occasionally you have an idea that’s different from what other people believe is right. You are never going to discover that unless you keep going with your beliefs, until you discover why they’re wrong. You should just ignore what other people say. I’m very good at ignoring what other people say.”

— Geoffrey Hinton

ironic because it is advice about ignoring advice. but I think it holds up, and I think the reason it holds up is not talked about enough.

the asymmetry

there is something that isn’t mentioned enough, or isn’t noticed: the asymmetry of information about yourself between you and other people. I first dove into this idea when learning about the efficient market hypothesis, which assumes the players in the market have symmetric information (I believe Jim Simons and Medallion are a pretty definite counterexample to the hypothesis, by the way).

the central idea is that if you are 17 years old and deciding what to work on, you should mostly ignore your English teacher, independently of whether you like their advice or not. this applies to counsellors, other teachers, and almost to parents as well. they do not know you as well as you do. your mother may know more facts about you, but you are the best positioned to assess anything about your 17-year-old self. the asymmetry in information is just insane.

think about what you have access to that nobody else does: what you think about in the shower, which problems you keep coming back to without being asked, what bores you even when you are good at it, what you would do on a free saturday. this is exactly the information that matters most for deciding what to work on, and it is almost invisible from the outside. a teacher sees your grades and maybe an hour a week of you, you see all of it.

what other people do know

this does not mean other people know nothing. they often know things about the world that you don’t: how a field actually works day to day, what happened to people who tried something similar, which paths close doors and which ones don’t. that is real information and it is worth asking for.

so the way I see it is you should separate the facts from the verdict. take the facts — “most phd students in this area do x”, “this program is very competitive” — and leave the verdict — “so you should do y” — because the verdict needs the information about you that only you have.

putting it together

this is where Hinton’s advice fits. if something seems right to you and everyone around you disagrees, the asymmetry is a reason to not drop it immediately, they might simply be missing information you have. but it is not a reason to stop thinking. keep going with it until you either find out why it is wrong, or you don’t.

most of the time you will find out why it is wrong, and that is fine, you learned something. but very occasionally you won’t, and those are the decisions that end up mattering.
