notes on linear algebra

linear algebra is an awesome course. it is the foundation for pretty much everything: calculus gives you tools, but the problems I believe most people encounter later in their academic careers (upper division, graduate, research, etc.) are embodied in linear algebra.

think about it: a dataset is a matrix, a neural network is mostly matrix multiplications with something nonlinear in between, a quantum state is a vector, and in fourier analysis a function becomes a vector in an infinite-dimensional inner product space, I remember thinking in complex analysis that multiplication is just linear transformations that only rotate and/or scale. once you see it, it is everywhere. 

on understanding

it is a great course to actually listen to and understand everything. get super familiar with vector spaces, inner product spaces, all spaces in general; get very comfortable with them and build intuition. norms, eigenvalues, orthogonality, everything — try to understand it. keep it to ℝ2, get a pencil and draw until you get it.

the one idea I would make sure to get is that a matrix is a linear map: it is a function that moves space around while keeping lines straight and the origin fixed. after that, most of the course is asking good questions about that map: what does it squash to zero (the kernel), what can it reach (the image), which directions does it only stretch (eigenvectors), and how much does it change areas (the determinant).

I think this picture is beautiful. a lot of things that look like separate chapters (eigenvalues, orthogonality, symmetric matrices, quadratic forms) are really the same picture seen from different angles. and once you have it in ℝ2, going to ℝn is mostly trusting that it still works.

also, prove things. linear algebra is, for some people, the first course where proofs feel natural (for me it was discrete mathematics), and it is a very good place to learn how to write them. proofs like "show that a set of orthonormal vectors are linearly independent" teach you more than ten computed examples.

on decompositions

do not pay much attention to decompositions like SVD, QR, LU, etc. memorise whichever technique your course tests and get it right on the final. whichever you need in the future you can re-learn, and if you need it outside of school you will just import it from a library.

on resources

I really recommend watching 3Blue1Brown's essence of linear algebra, as well as everything out there from Gilbert Strang. you have your own class, but if you ever want a second round of teaching on a concept he is a good reference, and he also has a textbook.
